Klopdit's brand guide

What the bill checker looks and sounds like, and what is fixed. You picked the meter's wheels on 22 November 2026. Every store image, card and flyer has been redrawn in it, and the name and icon are now outlines that need no font.

Klopdit is still the working name; that pick is open. If the name changes, the wheels take the new letters and everything else here holds. All readings and accounts in the pictures are examples.

The idea

Most of a water bill comes from one object: the meter outside, with its row of number wheels. The City of Cape Town's own page says "On all traditional water meters, black numbers represent kilolitres and red numbers represent litres." The brand is those wheels. The name is set in them, four black and three red, and the app's icon is one wheel that has rolled to a tick. No rival has a mark at all, and none uses the meter.

The mark

The wordmark is never smaller than 120 pixels wide, because below that the letters fill in; the icon takes over there, and it reads down to 16 pixels. Leave one wheel's width of clear space around either. The first four wheels are always white with ink letters and the last three always red with white letters.

Never stretch or tilt the mark, never change which wheels are red, never add a shadow or a glow, and never set the name in wheels of another colour.

Every text pair passes WCAG AA for normal text, 4.5 to 1, worked out from the values. Red is part of the brand, so it is never an alarm. A line that doesn't match gets the red tint and two words, "look here", never a red screen. A jump in use where the sums are right is blue, because nothing is wrong. In the dark, blue becomes #6aa8f5 and red #f08a78, both 7.6 to 1 on the ground; this page shows them if your device is set to dark.

Type

Schibsted Grotesk sets the words: weight 800 with tight spacing for headings, 400 for reading, 600 on buttons.

38 kL   R 1 204,60   0014

Martian Mono sets every number, reading and amount, and the two status words. It never sets a paragraph.

A picture holds at most three sizes of text, and no label is set in spaced-out capitals.

The wheels in the app

A meter reading is always shown on four wheels, so the difference between what was billed and what the meter says needs no explaining. While a bill is being checked the wheels may roll and settle on the result. With reduced motion switched on, they don't move.

How it talks

Like a neighbour who is good with numbers: calm, exact, on your side, never angry at the municipality. The sum comes before the opinion. Afrikaans leads in Swartland and Overstrand, and English sits beside it.

It always says

  • An example is an example.
  • A draft letter, for you to check and send.
  • Information, not legal advice.
  • Klopdit is not a municipality or part of one.

It never says

  • You'll get money back.
  • Your municipality is overcharging you.
  • Anything like "AI lawyer".
  • "Free", "best" or "new" on a store image.

Google Play asks an app that passes on government information to name its sources in the listing and to make clear it "doesn't represent a government or political entity". So the store listing links each municipality's tariff book, and the "not a municipality" line stays on everything.

The images, redrawn

Each is drawn at the size it is used, by a script kept beside it, so a change of name or words redraws all of them.

Still to check

Nobody has yet looked at this beside the three municipalities' logos: their sites refused the image files, so that needs your eyes or a screenshot of each. The one thing seen is that Swartland's site sets a dark green, #004d40, as its colour, which is far from ours. The Afrikaans wording needs a first-language reader. Apple's screenshot sizes and the two fonts' licences were not read today.
